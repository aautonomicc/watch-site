Security & your keys,
in plain English

W@tch has no accounts, no passwords and no servers — so the only secrets that matter are the ones that live on your own devices. This page explains exactly what they are, where they're kept, and what each one can do.

The short version The keys Self-generated wallet Trezor hardware wallet Trezor homescreens Good habits

Just watching needs none of this — no key, no wallet, no sign-up. Keys only enter the picture when you upload your own media (uploads are paid), back up your W@tch to the network, or link devices with My W@tch. Everything below is about those three things.

The short version

Five sentences that cover almost everything.

  1. Watching is key-free

    Streaming, downloading and browsing never touch a key or a wallet. There is no account to create, nothing to log into, and W@tch sends no telemetry.

  2. One wallet, your choice of two homes for it

    Uploads and backups are paid for from a small built-in wallet. Its key can live in software on your computer (the self-generated wallet) or inside a Trezor hardware wallet, where it never leaves the device. Both are explained below.

  3. 12 words rule everything

    The wallet is defined by a standard 12-word recovery phrase. Those words restore the money and — via W@tch's encrypted backup — your whole library, watch points and profiles on a fresh install. Anyone who has the words has all of that too, so they belong on paper, not in a file or photo.

  4. Backups are encrypted before they leave your device

    A backup is sealed with keys derived from your wallet — the network only ever stores ciphertext at an address nobody can derive without your words. Reading your backup back is free and needs no funded wallet.

  5. Device sync is sealed end to end

    My W@tch links your devices with a shared link secret (the invite code). Everything they exchange — library, watch points, profiles, backup read keys — travels encrypted under that secret. Treat invite codes like keys: share them only with your own devices.

  1. What never exists at all

    No W@tch server holds anything — there are no W@tch servers. No password database, no account recovery, no company that can reset, read or lose your keys. The flip side: nobody can recover them for you either. The 12 words on paper are the safety net.

The self-generated wallet

Created inside W@tch in a minute — the right choice for most people.

  1. How it's created

    W@tch generates a fresh 12-word phrase on your own device, shows it once, and makes you retype three of the words before anything is saved — proof you actually wrote them down. The phrase itself is never stored anywhere; only the key derived from it is kept. You can also import an existing 12- or 24-word phrase or a raw private key.

  2. Where the key lives

    In your operating system's keychain wherever one exists (macOS Keychain, Windows Credential Manager, the Linux Secret Service), so it's protected the same way your other saved credentials are. Where no keychain is available, W@tch falls back to a key file readable only by your user account — and tells you so on the wallet screen.

  3. Optional: lock the key file with a passphrase

    If your wallet uses the file fallback, you can protect it with a passphrase. The file is then encrypted with a deliberately slow key-stretching algorithm (Argon2id) and authenticated encryption (ChaCha20-Poly1305) — after every restart W@tch asks for the passphrase before the wallet unlocks. Forget the passphrase and the 12 words still restore everything.

  4. Treat it as a hot wallet

    A software wallet's key is on an internet-connected computer, which is exactly what "hot wallet" means. That's fine for its job — keep only small amounts in it, like cash in a pocket, and top it up when you upload. Never move savings into it. If you want the key off the computer entirely, that's what the Trezor option is for.

  5. What the 12 words restore

    On any fresh install: import the words, and the wallet is back. If you've used Settings → Backup, the same words also decrypt and restore your whole W@tch — library, watch points, profiles and artwork. One phrase, everything back.

Trezor hardware wallet

Keys that never touch the computer, and payments that need your finger.

  1. What a hardware wallet changes

    With a Trezor connected (desktop only, plain USB — no extra software or browser bridge needed), the wallet key is generated inside the device and never leaves it. W@tch sends the thing that needs signing over USB; you check it on the Trezor's own screen and confirm with a physical press; only the signature comes back. Malware on the computer can't spend your money — it has nothing to steal and can't press the button.

  2. The setup ceremony

    Connecting a Trezor to W@tch takes three confirmations on the device: it signs a fixed identity message twice (the two signatures must match exactly — a guard against a misbehaving device), and then shows the wallet address on its own screen so you can compare it with what W@tch displays. That last step matters: the Trezor's screen is the one display no software on your computer can fake. W@tch also verifies that the device's signature mathematically leads back to the address it reports, before anything is saved.

  3. Every payment is a tap

    Uploads and backups each wait for a physical confirmation on the device, with the amount shown on its screen — one tap per payment. W@tch opens one session with the device at the start of each backup or upload run (so a passphrase, if you use one, is entered once per run, and a missing or wrong device fails in seconds rather than after minutes of work), silently re-checks the address before each tap, and verifies every signature locally before anything is sent to the network — if something doesn't line up, nothing is sent and nothing is spent. Each tap comes with a plain-language prompt on screen and a short chime, so you know when the device is waiting for you. Whole upload batches are pooled so a batch typically needs just one payment confirmation.

  4. What's stored on the computer

    Only a public marker — the wallet address and a fingerprint, no secret of any kind. Optionally (on by default) W@tch keeps the derived backup read keys on the computer so your linked devices can follow your backups without the Trezor plugged in; those keys can read backups but can never spend. Turn that off and even reading a backup needs the device.

  5. Passphrase / hidden wallets work

    If your Trezor uses a passphrase (the "25th word" that opens a hidden wallet), enter it on the device as usual, once at the start of each backup or upload run — W@tch binds itself to whichever wallet the ceremony ran in, and refuses loudly, before any tap, if the device is later opened on a different hidden wallet. Each passphrase is its own wallet with its own backup line.

  6. Linux note

    The first connection on Linux may need Trezor's standard udev rules so your user can talk to the device — W@tch detects this and shows a dialog with the one-liner to copy. It's the usual Trezor setup step, not a W@tch quirk.

Good habits

The whole checklist, in one place.

  1. Words on paper

    Write the 12 words with a pen and keep them somewhere safe. No photos, no files, no cloud notes — they restore your money and decrypt your backups.

  2. Fund small

    A software wallet is a pocket, not a vault. Keep just enough for your next uploads; a feature film typically costs well under 1 ANT to store.

  3. Trust the Trezor's screen, not the computer's

    When a hardware wallet shows an address or an amount, that display is the truth. If it ever disagrees with what W@tch shows, stop and press nothing.

  4. Invite codes are keys

    A My W@tch invite lets a device read everything your devices sync. Only ever link devices you own, and show codes to no one else.

  5. Remember it's alpha software

    W@tch is built in the open and moves fast. The code is public on GitHub for anyone to inspect — but alpha means alpha: fund small, keep the words safe, update every linked device together.
